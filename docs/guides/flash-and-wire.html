Bench procedure · Steps 2 and 3

Flash and Wire the ESP32

Getting the supervisor firmware onto the board, and the board wired to the probe, the fob and the button. All of it happens on the bench, with the saw disconnected. Nothing here goes near the motor or the mains.

BoardESP32-DevKitC-32E

This page expands Steps 2 and 3 of NEXT-STEPS.md into the actual keystrokes and the actual wires. The sources of record are firmware/README.md for the flashing and hardware/schematic/WIRING.md for the wiring — if this page and one of those ever disagree, they win.

It is written for someone who is not an electronics person, working with the project owner on a call. The rule from the procedure holds everywhere below: if anything does not go the way the step says, stop and ask. Not "try the next thing." Nothing here is urgent, and a guess is expensive.

Two things that stay true the whole time
  • The saw's disconnect is locked off and the key is in your pocket. Even though this is bench work, the box gets opened in the same session.
  • Dropping the contactor does not make the enclosure safe. An accessory 240 V outlet was added ahead of everything protective and stays live. Only the disconnect kills it.

Before you start

Part A

Loading the firmware

One script does the whole job. It installs what it needs, runs its own tests, finds the board, reads the real flash size off the chip and picks the matching layout. Your job is to read what it prints.

A1

Plug the board into the Mac

USB-C, straight into the laptop — not through a hub. A hub that cannot supply enough current shows up later as a corrupted upload, which is a confusing failure to chase.

Nothing else is connected to the board. No probe, no fob, no wires.

A2

Ask the script what it can see

Open a terminal in the project folder and run:

terminal
$ cd firmware
$ ./scripts/flash.sh doctor

The first run installs PlatformIO, which takes a few minutes and prints a lot. That is normal. What matters is the four lines at the end:

what to look for
board
  port         /dev/cu.usbserial-0001
  flash        8 MB (read from the chip)
build
  environment  path_a_8mb
  generated    generated/error_codes.h present

A port means the laptop can talk to the board. The flash size is read off the chip, not guessed, and that is what picks the environment — an 8 MB layout on a 4 MB chip does not boot at all, so the script refuses to guess.

Done when

doctor prints a port and an environment starting with path_a.

Stop and ask if

It says no serial port found. Nine times out of ten that is a charge-only cable — try another one before anything else. If a second cable does not fix it, the board's USB chip needs a driver on this Mac, and that is a job for the guide, not a thing to keep re-running.

A3

Run the tests before you write anything

terminal
$ ./scripts/flash.sh test

76 tests, about a second, no board needed. They exercise the part of the code that decides whether the saw stops: every state transition, the sensor-fault rules, the temperature maths, the lockout counter.

Stop and ask if

Any test fails. Do not flash a red build onto a saw. There is nothing to work around here — a failure means the logic that stops the motor is not behaving, and that is the whole product.

A4

Write it to the board

terminal
$ ./scripts/flash.sh flash

It stops and asks you to confirm. Read what it is asking, because it is a real question and not a formality:

the confirmation
About to write firmware to the board.
  The supervisor cannot protect the saw while it is being flashed.
  GPIO26 must have its 10 kOhm pulldown fitted, or the machine
  disconnect must be locked out. See WIRING.md.

  Continue? [y/N]

Uploading resets the board, and during the reset its pins are floating — the chip is not driving them either way. On a board sitting bare on a bench that means nothing. On a board already wired into the saw it means the transmit pin is undefined, which is exactly what the pulldown resistor in Part B exists to make safe.

Today the board is on the bench and unwired, so answer y.

One command instead of three

./scripts/flash.sh all chains doctor, then test, then flash. Same steps, same confirmation. Use it once you have seen each piece work on its own.

A5

Read the boot banner

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that tells you whether the board is actually working.

terminal
$ ./scripts/flash.sh monitor

Press the board's EN button if nothing appears — the banner prints once, at startup, and you may have connected after it went by. Press ctrl + ] to get out again.

boot banner — abridged
=== table saw thermal supervisor ===

primary sensor  frame NTC
trip / warn / reset   90.0 / 78.0 / 55.0 C (frame temperature)
cooldown hold   120 s

reset reason    power-on
calibration     *** DEFAULTS IN USE ***

Four things to check, and to say out loud on the call:

Reading the banner
LineWhat it should say, and what it means if it doesn't
pathMust read A (frame NTC, RF heartbeat). Path B is for hardware this project has not bought — flashed by mistake, it reports a sensor fault forever, because there is no thermocouple amplifier on the bus to answer it.
self-test8 or more valid reads out of 10. Fewer means the firmware refused to arm because it does not trust the probe. With no probe connected yet, expect this to fail — see the note below.
reset reasonpower-on right after a flash. On a later boot, TASK WATCHDOG or panic is a real defect and gets chased, not ignored.
calibrationDEFAULTS IN USE is expected and correct today. It means the two-point calibration has not been done on this board, so its temperatures are unverified. The board works; its numbers are not yet trustworthy.
A failed self-test right now is the right answer

With no probe wired, the sensor pin reads nothing believable, so the firmware holds the contact open and puts the LED on SOS. That is the fail-safe behaviour working. Come back and re-read this banner after Part B, with the probe plugged in — that is when the self-test has to pass.

Done when

The banner prints, it says path A, and the reset reason is power-on.

A6

Learn the LED, because it is the only display at the machine

Once the enclosure is closed there is no serial log and probably no phone in hand. The onboard blue LED is how an operator knows what the supervisor is doing.

LED patterns
PatternStateMeaning
Solid ONARMEDReady. Press Start.
Slow blink (1 Hz)COOLDOWNWait for solid.
Fast blink (5 Hz)TRIPPED — thermalMotor overtemperature.
Double-blink then pauseTRIPPED — sensor faultCheck sensor / wiring.
Triple-blink then long pauseMANUAL_LOCKOUTToo many trips. Press ack after cooling to clear.

From a cold boot the board sits in COOLDOWN for two minutes before it goes solid. That wait is deliberate. A slow blink straight after flashing is not a fault.

A7

Optional — put the fault pages on the board

terminal
$ ./scripts/flash.sh fs

This uploads the operator fault pages into the board's own storage, so a phone standing at the saw with no internet can still read what an error code means. Skip it today if you like; it can be run any time.

Either way, the dashboard is there once the board is running: join the Wi-Fi network named ALN Table Saw and open http://saw.local/. It shows state, temperature and the thresholds this board is actually running. It cannot change anything — the network is deliberately advisory only, and there is no path from a web page to the pin that stops the saw.

When it goes wrong

Flashing — the usual failures
What you seeWhat it is
No ESP32 serial port foundCharge-only cable, almost always. Try another. If that fails, the USB-to-serial chip needs a driver installed on this Mac.
Timed out waiting for packet headerThe board is not listening. Hold BOOT, tap EN, release BOOT, run the command again.
the port is busyA serial monitor is still open in another window. Close it — monitor holds the port.
MD5 of file does not matchBad cable or an underpowered hub. Different cable, straight into the laptop.
Boots, then reboots foreverWrong partition layout for the chip. Run doctor to see the real flash size, then flash with --env path_a, which is safe on either size.
Serial shows nothing at allThe board is not running. Look at the LED: an SOS pattern means the boot self-test failed, which is a wiring answer, not a flashing one.
Two things never to do to make a flash succeed
  • Never turn off a safety check to get a build through — not the watchdog, not the boot self-test, not the sensor validation. If one of them is in the way, it is telling you something true.
  • Never raise the trip temperature to stop nuisance trips. Repeated trips mean the fan shroud is packed with sawdust, which is the exact failure this whole project exists to catch.

Part B

Wiring it up

Six connections, done slowly and one at a time with the guide reading from WIRING.md. Two of them are measurements rather than wires, and they come first, because the numbers they produce decide what the later steps are allowed to do.

Part B cannot start yet — four parts are missing

The bench has the ESP32, the temperature probe and the fob. Every one of Part B's three circuits also needs something that is not on the bench, and none of the three has a workaround:

RefPartWithout it
R1~10 kΩ resistor, 1%The temperature probe cannot be read at all. A thermistor on its own produces no voltage — it needs a fixed resistor above it to divide against. There is nothing on the chip to borrow: GPIO34 is input-only, and this board's input-only pins have no internal resistors.
R210 kΩ resistorNo pulldown on GPIO26. This is the one item WIRING.md calls mandatory with no substitute — see step B3.
Q1PC817 optocouplerNo safe way to drive the fob. The rule does not bend: no ESP32 pin touches a fob pad directly.
R3330 Ω resistorNothing limiting current into the optocoupler's input LED.

Any assorted resistor kit covers R1, R2 and R3; only Q1 is a real purchase, and a 2N3904 or 2N7000 substitutes if no optocoupler turns up (WIRING.md gives both, and says why they are second choice). Get R1 in 1% — it is the reference every reported temperature is divided against, and it is the number step B2 tells you to measure.

Part A above needs none of this. Flashing the board takes the ESP32, a laptop and a USB-C cable, and it is worth doing now: it proves the board is good and gets the failure modes out of the way while the parts are on order.

Two things on this sheet the rest of the repository no longer agrees with
  • It shows no acknowledge button, and no GPIO27. Its legend says so outright. That was true when it was drawn and is not true now: the firmware fits the button on both build paths, because a persisted lockout survives a power cycle and a board with no ack input would have no way out of one. Step B6 below is right; the sheet is behind.
  • It labels the fob as a 12 V A23 cell, and its warning box gives 12 V as the reason not to wire GPIO26 straight to the pad. The board on the bench has a coin-cell holder instead, and nobody has metered it. The warning's conclusion still stands — the reason attached to it does not. That is what step B1 exists to settle. not measured
B1

Measure the fob's battery voltage — before anything touches it

Meter on DC volts, red probe to the fob's red wire, black to black. Say the number out loud and write it down.

This is a gate, not a formality. The project's older documents assumed the fob ran on a 12 V cell; the board actually on the bench has a coin-cell holder, which is a different number. Nobody has measured it. The ESP32's pins are destroyed by anything above 3.3 V, permanently and instantly.

The rule that follows from not knowing

No ESP32 pin ever connects to a fob pad directly. Not because 12 V is certain, but because the number is unknown. Everything between them goes through the level shifter in step B5.

Done when

The cell voltage is written down, and the guide has recorded it in WIRING.md. not measured yet

B2

Measure the divider resistor, and work out the fob's buttons

Two more numbers that do not exist yet.

The divider resistor. Meter on ohms, across the resistor before it is fitted. It is sold as 10 kΩ but a cheap one really measures anywhere from 9.5 kΩ to 10.5 kΩ, and that error goes straight into every temperature the board reports. Write down the actual number; it gets typed into the firmware's calibration file.

The fob's button pads. Six wires come out of the fob: red and black at the battery, and a pair at each of the two buttons — yellow with orange, blue with green. Which pair is the ON button, and which wire of that pair the fob's own chip is watching, is read off a photograph and nothing more. Meter it out with the guide before wiring anything to it.

Done when

Three numbers are on paper: the resistor's real value, which pair is ON, and which wire of that pair is the one the fob reads. not measured yet

B3

Fit the pulldown resistor first — before any other wire

A 10 kΩ resistor between GPIO26 and GND. Fit it now, ahead of everything, and measure across it afterwards to confirm about 10 kΩ.

Here is what it does. GPIO26 is the pin that holds the fob transmitting, and transmitting is what lets the saw run. While the board is starting up, or has crashed, or is browning out, the chip is not driving that pin at all — it floats, and a floating pin can read as "on."

The resistor is what makes floating mean off. It quietly pulls the pin down to ground whenever nothing is driving it, so a crashed or half-booted supervisor stops transmitting, the receiver's contact opens, and the saw stops.

This one has no substitute

Without it, every claim this project makes about failing safely is untrue. It is also what makes it safe to re-flash the board once it is installed in the enclosure. Fit it first so that nothing you wire afterwards is ever briefly unprotected.

Done when

The meter reads roughly 10 kΩ between GPIO26 and GND, with the board unpowered.

B4

Build a common ground, then the temperature divider

Pick one GND pin on the ESP32 as the ground bus. Everything that needs a ground lands there. The board has several GND pins and they are all the same node — if you use more than one, tie them together so there is no doubt.

Then the probe circuit, which is three connections in a line:

NTC divider — 3V3 → 10 kΩ → GPIO34 → probe → GND
FromToNotes
Top of the 10 kΩ resistor orange3V3The board's own regulated supply. Never connect an outside 3.3 V here.
Junction of resistor and probe purpleGPIO34This pin and no other. See the warning below.
Other side of the probe blackground busThe probe is a thermistor — it has no polarity, so either of its two wires may go either way.

The probe plugs in. Its factory connector now has its mate fitted, so there is nothing to cut and nothing to solder. Keep that plug inside the enclosure rather than out on the motor, where dust and vibration would work it loose.

Why GPIO34 specifically

The ESP32 has two banks of measuring pins, and one of them stops working the moment Wi-Fi turns on — those pins simply read zero. GPIO34 is on the bank that keeps working. Several other pins look interchangeable and are not. Moving the probe to GPIO25, 26, 27, 4, 12–15, 0 or 2 produces a board that reads zero degrees forever.

Done when

The probe is plugged in and monitor shows the self-test passing — 8 or more valid reads out of 10 — with a temperature that looks like the room. Pinch the probe tip between two fingers: it should climb toward 30–35 °C and fall back when you let go.

B5

Put the level shifter between GPIO26 and the fob

This is the piece that lets the ESP32 press the fob's button without the two circuits touching electrically. An optocoupler is the recommended part: inside it, the ESP32 lights a tiny LED, and that light closes a switch on the fob's side. No shared wire, no shared ground, and the unknown cell voltage from step B1 cannot reach the ESP32 at all.

Optocoupler — recommended
Optocoupler pinGoes toNotes
A — anodeGPIO26 through a 330 Ω resistor brownThe resistor limits the current into the internal LED.
K — cathodeground bus black
C — collectorthe ON pad the fob's chip watchesWhich one that is comes from step B2's meter reading.
E — emitterthe other ON padBoth pads are on the pigtail, so nothing has to be tied to fob ground.

If no optocoupler can be found, a small transistor will do the same job and WIRING.md gives that version. It works, and it is second choice for a reason: it needs the two grounds tied together, which puts that unmeasured cell voltage one mistake away from the ESP32.

Handle the fob by the edges of the green board

Those six solder joints are somebody's finished work. A tug on a wire lifts the pad off the board, and that is a repair, not an adjustment.

B6

Fit the acknowledge button and check the LED

The button is a plain momentary switch: one side to GPIO27, the other to the ground bus. No resistor — the chip supplies its own internally, so the pin sits high until a press pulls it low.

Any scrounged pushbutton works. So does touching a bare wire from GPIO27 to GND, which is enough to finish commissioning before a proper button is mounted.

This button matters more than its size suggests. After three trips inside ten minutes the supervisor stops resetting itself and waits for a person — and that lockout is written into the board's memory, so power-cycling does not clear it. The button is the only way out. A press is only accepted once the motor has actually cooled; pressed while it is still hot, it is logged and ignored on purpose.

The LED is already on the board at GPIO2 and needs no wire. Check that it is really there — a few board revisions fit only a power LED. If yours has no blue LED that blinks, wire one from GPIO2 through a 330 Ω resistor to ground, because without it the operator has no indicator at the machine at all.

Done when

Pressing the button changes nothing visible yet — correct, since the board is not in lockout. The guide will exercise it properly during the bench tests.

B7

Power it from a proper USB charger

The board is powered through its own USB-C socket, from an ordinary listed USB phone charger. There is no power wire to run and nothing lands on the VIN pin.

That choice is doing real safety work. A listed charger is a certified supply with proper isolation built in, which keeps the mains side of this machine and the low-voltage side genuinely separate. The unidentified "220 to 12 V converter" in the parts pile is not a substitute and must not be used.

One decision still open

Which outlet the charger plugs into has not been settled. A separate wall outlet leaves the supervisor powered after the machine disconnect is opened; the new accessory receptacle ties it to the disconnect instead. Both are defensible. What is not defensible is drawings saying one and the enclosure doing the other, so whichever gets built has to be written into the build log. not decided

The receiver is not on this page

The radio receiver lives on the mains side of the machine, inside the control box, and wiring it is a separate job done with the disconnect locked out. Two things about it are worth knowing now, because both are easy to get backwards:

  • Do not follow the manufacturer's own wiring diagram. It runs the receiver straight to the contactor coil. On a pump that is fine. On a saw it removes the interlock that keeps the machine stopped after an interruption — the saw would restart by itself the moment the radio link recovered.
  • The receiver has to be set to momentary mode, and then proven by watching it: hold a fob button and its switch must close, let go and it must open. If its switch is closed while nothing is transmitting, stop. That inverts the entire safety design, and everything else here depends on it being the other way round. not verified

What comes next

A flashed, wired board is not a protected saw

Finishing both parts above gets you to the start of the work that actually proves something. Be blunt about this on the call, because "it's wired" reads to everyone else as "it's protected."

The next step is Step 4 — prove it stops the saw, still with the machine disconnected. Three tests, and every one of them must make the receiver's switch click open:

  1. Heat the probe with a hair dryer or hot water. It should trip at its temperature limit.
  2. Pull the ESP32's power while it is armed. The switch must open within about a second.
  3. Unplug the probe while it is running. The switch must open.

If all three do what they should, the fail-safe idea is real on this bench. If any one of them does not, the build stops there until it is understood.

Still true after all of that

  • The passive backstop has not been bought. The design calls for a bimetallic thermostat that cuts the motor on temperature with no electronics involved. It is not in the box. Until it is, every protective function depends on this board continuing to run.
  • The temperature limits are placeholders. The real numbers come from watching the motor through a normal session and setting the limits around what it actually does. That has not happened.
  • The firmware has never run on this motor. The tests prove the logic behaves. They prove nothing about this probe, this motor or this receiver.
  • The fan shroud still has to be cleaned and the starter's overload heaters still have to be checked. That blocked shroud is the original fault. A monitor installed over an uncleaned one will faithfully report a motor cooking itself.

So: finish these steps, then treat the saw as instrumented, not as protected, until the owner says otherwise. Do not cut wood.
