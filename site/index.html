Where this actually stands

The starter is wired in its enclosure. The probe and the RF fob are connectorised. The firmware is written and green in CI. None of it has been commissioned.

No firmware has been flashed. Nothing has run on hardware. Not one fault test has been performed, no threshold has been set from a measurement, and the passive thermostat that the design calls its primary protection has not been bought yet. The repository keeps a separate build log for exactly this reason — every other document in it describes an intended state, and it is very easy to read a finished-sounding design document as a finished machine.

So this page describes a design and an assembly in progress, and says so in the same voice it uses for everything else. The saw is not cutting wood.

§ 1  The failure

A protector that worked until it wore out

The saw stopped starting. The original protection was a Klixon BEC2921 — a manual-reset snap-action bimetal device with a red pop-out plunger, wired in series with one motor line and carrying full motor current. It tested open with the motor cold, and continuity did not come back after resetting the plunger. The windings tested good on every reading. The protector itself had failed.

Two things about that arrangement are worth naming, because the replacement is shaped by both.

It carried the inrush. Locked-rotor current on this motor runs somewhere around 90–115 A, and every single start pushed that through the protector’s contacts. Thousands of cycles plus repeated thermal trips wore the mechanism out.

It sat downstream of the contactor. When it opened, the motor stopped — but the contactor stayed latched in and the Start button stayed sealed in. Cool the protector, reset it, and the motor could resume with nobody touching the machine. Protection was interrupting the load instead of controlling the starter.

§ 2  Root cause

The fan shroud was packed solid with sawdust

A TEFC motor — totally enclosed, fan cooled — sheds heat one way only: air pushed over its finned frame by a shroud-mounted fan. Block the shroud and the motor cooks at its rated current. This one has a service factor of 1.0, which means no thermal margin at all: rated load is the ceiling.

The Klixon had been doing its job, repeatedly, for as long as it could. It wore out doing it.

That diagnosis decided the whole shape of the replacement, because of one consequence:

The consequence

Current sensing would never have caught this. The starter’s overload heaters see amps, not degrees — and the motor was drawing perfectly normal current the entire time it was overheating.

Temperature sensing is the point of the entire project. Everything else follows from it.

There is a prerequisite that goes with it, and it is not optional: clean the shroud, and verify the overload heaters are sized correctly before any of this is fitted. With 14.4 A full-load current and SF 1.0, code caps overload protection at 115 % — 16.5 A. If a previous owner oversized them, the current-protection half is already compromised, and no amount of temperature sensing fixes that. A monitoring system installed under the same cooling conditions will faithfully report a motor cooking itself.

§ 3  Topology

Nothing in this system can start the saw

The replacement moves protection off the motor line and onto the contactor coil rung — the low-current control circuit that decides whether the starter pulls in. Both protective layers are normally-closed contacts in series with the coil. Opening either one drops the contactor and stops the motor.

The saw’s existing 3-wire control — momentary Start, maintained Stop, seal-in auxiliary contact — is load-bearing here. Because the seal-in drops with the coil, a recovered fault re-closes the relay but cannot restart the motor. Someone has to walk over and press Start. That property is a consequence of the wiring, not of anything the firmware promises.

The supervisor’s contact is normally open and energised to close. That polarity is the whole safety argument: losing power, losing firmware execution, or tripping the watchdog all de-energise it, open the rung, and stop the saw. There is no failure of the ESP32 that leaves the saw running unprotected — the interesting failures all stop it instead.

And nothing in the system connects to anything that could energise the coil. The only electrical function this project is permitted to perform is opening a series contact. That is verified by looking at wiring, not by reading firmware.

§ 4  Two builds

What can be built tonight, and what it becomes

Verifying the bill of materials turned up two problems. The NTC probe that was bought tops out at 125 °C, which sits inside the decision band rather than above it — and its PVC lead would not survive against a winding anyway. The “wireless switch” turned out to be a fob-driven RF receiver, not a GPIO-controllable relay.

Rather than leave the saw dead waiting on parts, the project splits: a same-day build from what is on the bench, and the end-state design it grows into. Both honour the same requirements.

Path A

Being built now

Protection from parts on hand, fail-safe by heartbeat. The ESP32 transmits continuously through the 433 MHz fob to a receiver programmed in momentary mode. Anything that stops the transmission — power loss, firmware hang, sensor fault, watchdog reset, even jamming — drops the receiver’s relay and stops the saw.

Sensor
The repurposed NTC, on the motor frame rather than the winding. Advisory precision, but the delta between a healthy frame and an unhealthy one is exactly what needs detecting.
Isolation
A UL-listed USB phone charger — a certified reinforced-isolation supply that anyone can buy.
Passive layer
None. Everything protective depends on the ESP32 continuing to transmit.
Character
Nuisance-stop-prone by design. That is the correct direction to err — the alternative is running unprotected.

Path B

Design of record

Two genuinely independent layers, both in series with the coil. If every wire to the ESP32 is pulled out, the motor is still thermally protected — that independence is the point of the whole exercise, and it is the thing Path A does not yet have.

Layer 1
A passive bimetallic snap-action thermostat, normally closed, opening on rise, auto-reset. Primary protection. No firmware involved.
Layer 2
K-type thermocouple bonded to the winding end turns through an aluminium-nitride substrate, read through a MAX31855, driving a wired opto-isolated relay straight off a GPIO. No RF anywhere in the safety path.
Ordering
The supervisor trips below the thermostat’s open temperature, so it normally acts first and logs the event. The thermostat is the unmonitored backstop.
New capability
Rate-of-rise tracking. A °C/min figure creeping up across sessions says the shroud is packing again — a warning before the trip, which is the one thing the original protector could never give.

The schematics in the repository are drawn only for the parts that exist. A sheet full of components nobody owns is how a builder ends up wiring the wrong thing, so Path B lives as prose and open tasks — with the thermostat’s landing point marked as a ghost slot on the ladder sheet, the same way it is drawn above.

§ 5  Thresholds

Four numbers, and where they came from

These are the Path B defaults, measured at the winding. They exist in exactly one place in the repository, and a CI job parses them out of the design document and fails the build if any operator-facing page quotes a number that disagrees. An operator reading a stale threshold off a screen at a machine is precisely the failure that pipeline exists to prevent.

Two more sit alongside them: a trip must stay below the reset threshold for a sustained 120 seconds before the machine re-arms, and three seconds without a valid reading is itself a trip.

Path A’s numbers are not these. The frame runs cooler than the winding, so its thresholds have to come from an observed baseline — run normal cuts, watch the steady-state frame temperature, and set the trip above it. That baseline run has not happened, which means every Path A threshold currently in the firmware is a placeholder. Each unit prints the thresholds it is actually running in its boot log and at the top of its dashboard, so the numbers at the machine are the ones to trust.

The thermostat’s 120–130 °C is a purchasing specification rather than a description. It has to open above the supervisor’s trip point: buying a 110 °C part would collapse the two layers into one, and the saw would then stop with no log entry, no error code and no rate-of-rise data — the exact failure mode the retrofit exists to escape.

§ 6  Firmware

The decisions that stop the saw run without an Arduino

Everything that decides whether the saw stops — the state machine, the sensor-validation rules, the threshold arithmetic, the trip counter, the LED patterns — lives in a protection core written as pure C++ with no Arduino dependency. It compiles and runs on a laptop, where 76 host unit tests exercise it before anything is flashed. The Arduino layer around it is drivers, tasks and the dashboard.

On the device, protection owns core 0 at the highest priority and never blocks on the network. Wi-Fi, the HTTP server and the log live on core 1 and are advisory only: the dashboard may display state and acknowledge an alert, and that is the entire list. It cannot lower a threshold, force-arm, or close the relay — enforced on the server, not just hidden in the UI. Wi-Fi down means the saw is still protected.

The watchdog is fed only at the end of a complete, successful cycle. A sensor read that hangs starves it, the chip resets, and the relay opens. That ordering is deliberate and it is the kind of thing that gets quietly “fixed” by someone trying to stop a reset.

And any doubt about a reading is a trip. Open sensor, shorted sensor, out of range, stale, bad CRC — all of them stop the saw. There is no last-known-good, no assume-ambient, and no clamping an impossible reading back into range.

What the operator sees at the machine

The dashboard may not be visible from where someone is standing, so the onboard LED is the primary indicator. Seven distinguishable patterns cover every state that matters — shown here running at their real rates.

Off and boot-failure are both fail-safe — the relay is open in each — but the LED distinguishes them, because one sends you to the power supply and the other sends you to the probe.

Three trips inside a rolling ten-minute window drops the machine into a manual lockout instead of auto-cycling. A glitchy sensor or an intermittent connection should not sit there chattering the coil relay open and closed every few minutes writing log spam. Clearing that lockout takes a physical button press, and only once the temperature is genuinely down; the network cannot clear it and neither can a power cycle, because the state persists across reboots.

One source for three artifacts

Every operator-visible fault has a stable code, written once as a Markdown page and built into three places: the pages on GitHub, an offline HTML bundle served from the saw’s own access point, and the firmware’s code table. Nothing is hand-copied. Codes are append-only — a published code never changes meaning and is never reused, because it may be printed on the card inside the cabinet door.

The distinction between E02 and E03 is whether the sensing chain answered, not how long it has been wrong. An amplifier that keeps reporting an open-circuit flag is answering every cycle, so it stays E02 however long it goes on — the fault is in the probe. A bus that has gone silent becomes E03, because then the fault is in the link. Those are two different things to go and look at.

§ 7  Requirements

Eight rules that are not up for negotiation

Any change that appears to violate one of these is wrong until proven otherwise. They are written down so that a later, tired, convenient decision has something to argue with.

SR-1

Fail-safe relay topology

Normally open, energised to close, in series with the coil. Losing power, losing firmware, or a watchdog reset stops the saw.

SR-2

Interrupt only

No output connects to anything that could energise the coil. Opening a series contact is the only permitted function. Verified by inspecting wiring.

SR-3  ·  unmet

Passive primary retained

A thermostat independent of the ESP32 is the primary layer. It is not fitted, so today the supervisor is not a supervisor — it is the only layer.

SR-4

No autonomous start

Recovery re-closes the relay and nothing more. Restarting takes a physical press of Start, guaranteed by the seal-in topology rather than by firmware.

SR-5

Sensor fault is a trip

Open, shorted, out of range, stale, bad CRC — all trip. Never fall back to last-known-good or assume ambient.

SR-6

Watchdog

Fed only after a successful read and evaluation. A hung sensor task must starve it rather than be papered over.

SR-7

Galvanic isolation

The sensor is bonded at winding potential. Everything on the low-voltage side must be isolated from mains before anything is energised.

SR-8

Network is advisory

The web interface may display and acknowledge. It may never be required for protection to work, and all network input is untrusted.

The outlet is the interesting one

A 240 V receptacle was added off the incoming supply, deliberately outside the protection — nothing plugged into it is stopped by the overload relay, the supervisor, or the thermostat that is still to come. That is a legitimate thing to want in a machine enclosure and a dangerous thing to leave undocumented, because a receptacle bolted to a saw reads to everyone else as part of the saw. It gets a label at the outlet.

It also forces a decision. Every drawing currently says the supervisor’s charger comes from a separate wall outlet, which means opening the machine disconnect leaves the supervisor powered. Feeding it from this new outlet instead ties supervisor power to the disconnect, which is what the design asks for. Either answer is defensible. What is not defensible is drawings that say one thing and an enclosure that does the other.

§ 9  Open

Measurements the build cannot be finished without

Each of these is a number nobody has yet, sitting underneath something that has already been written down as if it were known. Listing them is cheaper than discovering them at the machine.

The measured value of the NTC divider resistor. A 5 % part can read anywhere from 9.5 to 10.5 kΩ.

Blocks — every temperature the board reports

Two-point calibration of the frame probe, in ice water and boiling water, landing within ±2 °C.

Blocks — thresholds meaning anything

The fob’s rail voltage, and which of the six pigtail conductors goes to which pad.

Blocks — choosing the level shifter

Whether the receiver’s contact is open with nothing transmitting. If it is closed, the entire fail-safe premise is wrong and the build stops there.

Blocks — the whole heartbeat design

The receiver’s momentary-mode hold time.

Blocks — the heartbeat’s timing budget

The A202C’s coil voltage, read off the coil label.

Blocks — the receiver’s supply tap

The accessory outlet’s type, rating, conductor size, overcurrent protection, and whether it sits upstream or downstream of the machine disconnect.

Blocks — its own safety

The steady-state frame temperature through a normal cutting session — the baseline every Path A threshold is supposed to be set from.

Blocks — commissioning

And then ten commissioning tests, none of which have been run

Relay opens on power removal, on a forced crash, on a disconnected sensor, on a watchdog timeout. Trip at threshold under a heat gun. Two-point calibration. Isolation continuity check. Thermostat bench test before it goes anywhere near the motor. Contactor drops out on a trip and does not re-latch when it clears. Wi-Fi killed while armed, with the protection loop not caring. Three trips in ten minutes reaching lockout. Then a first run under load to record the baseline.

Until those pass, this is a well-documented assembly, not a protection system. Which is a fine thing to be — it just is not the other thing.

Scope

This retrofit modifies a UL-listed motor and voids its listing. For a private shop that is the owner’s call. If this saw were ever used in a commercial, institutional, insured-workshop or teaching setting, the correct answer is a listed replacement protector, and this project would not be appropriate.
